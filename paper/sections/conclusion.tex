\section{Conclusion}

This paper presents Boundary Density Likelihood as a direct way to train sequence models for event localization.
Rather than treating onsets and offsets as consequences of a predicted state mask, BDL makes them the supervised objects: each annotation is one expected event on the model timeline, and the model learns a nonnegative rate sequence whose peaks are scored as detections.
The likelihood framing gives the density target a fixed unit, so smoothing and downsampling can express timing uncertainty without changing the number of events being learned.

Across the sleep and CHB-MIT benchmarks, this objective improves event AP under matched architectures, scorers, and post-processing.
On sleep, the gain is largest at strict matching tolerances, persists across multiple backbones, and is not reproduced by segmentation reweighting or smoothing choices alone.
These are the settings where the distinction matters most: long recordings, long-duration states, sparse boundaries, and strict timing tolerances.
A model can describe the interior of an episode reasonably well while still ranking the clinically relevant endpoint poorly.
BDL improves those event-level scores without requiring a new backbone; it is mainly a change in what the sequence output is asked to estimate.

The state-reconstruction result is important because it marks the boundary of the claim.
If the deployed product is a sleep--wake mask or another samplewise state, segmentation remains the right objective.
If the deployed product is a ranked list of onsets, offsets, or alarms, BDL is the better matched training signal.
